# 3. WATER / hydrology layer

The Amazon Basin is the world's largest drainage basin. Central Amazonia includes terra-firme forest, seasonally flooded várzea and igapó forests, black-water and white-water rivers, lakes, channels, swamps, islands, and beaches.

Corpus primitive:

```text

::
CONNECT
::
FLOOD
::
DEPOSIT
::
TRANSPORT
::
RETURN
```

NASA's Amazon research also supports a strong land-atmosphere moisture-recycling loop:

```text
soil / river
   |
forest uptake
   |
transpiration
   |
atmosphere
   |
cloud / rain
   |
soil / river
```

**Corpus relation:** recurrence / carrier / network / homeostatic feedback.

# 4. EARLY CULTIVATION / plant knowledge

## Southwestern Amazonia / Llanos de Moxos

Archaeological evidence from Bolivia places manioc cultivation at about 10,350 calibrated years BP, squash at about 10,250 BP, and maize at about 6,850 BP. Human-created forest islands began forming by roughly 10,850 BP in the seasonally flooded savannah.

```text
PLANT
-> select / tend
-> repeat
-> landscape patch
-> persistent food system
```

## Upper Amazon cacao

Evidence from Santa Ana-La Florida in southeast Ecuador documents cacao use around 5,300 years ago using starch grains, theobromine residues, and ancient DNA. The upper Amazon is currently the oldest archaeologically demonstrated center of cacao use/domestication.

```text
CACAO
::
USE
::
SELECT
::
EXCHANGE
```

**Status:** SUPPORTED.

# 5. SOIL layer — terra preta / Amazonian Dark Earth

```text
ordinary tropical soil
 +
charcoal / organic refuse / repeated occupation
 ->
dark anthropogenic soil
 ->
greater nutrient retention
 ->
persistent cultivation potential
```

Smithsonian sources describe terra preta as fertile anthropogenic dark earth created by Indigenous land use, with charcoal-rich practices documented broadly between about 2,500 and 500 years ago.

Whether every occurrence was deliberately engineered as a designed soil recipe versus emerging from repeated habitation and cultivation can vary by site and interpretation.

**Corpus relation:** append / accumulation / memory in substrate.

# 6. GEOMETRY / EARTHWORK layer

## Acre geoglyphs

UNESCO's Brazilian tentative-list documentation records hundreds of geometric earthwork sites in Acre, including circular and square enclosures with ditches and low walls. Their construction required substantial planning, earth moving, geometry, drainage awareness, and durable landscape modification.

```text
LAND
-> MEASURE
-> CUT / BANK
-> CIRCLE / SQUARE
-> CONNECT
-> PERSIST
```

**Corpus relation:** geometry -> landscape -> long-term address.

# 7. SETTLEMENT / NETWORK layer

## Casarabe culture — southwest Amazonia, ca. AD 500-1400

LiDAR mapping in the Bolivian Amazon revealed a four-tier settlement system with large centers such as Cotoca and Landívar, platform mounds, pyramidal structures up to about 22 m high, polygonal enclosures, straight raised causeways, canals, and reservoirs.

```text
CENTER
  |
CAUSEWAY
  |
SECONDARY CENTER
  |
SMALLER SITE
  |
ACTIVITY NODE
```

plus:

```text
WATER
-> canal
-> reservoir
-> settlement
-> field
```

Nature characterizes this as a form of pre-Hispanic tropical low-density urbanism.

**Status:** SUPPORTED.

# 8. MARAJOARA layer — mouth of the Amazon

Marajó Island preserves a major archaeological ceramic tradition. Smithsonian/NMAI dates characteristic Marajoara material broadly to AD 400-1300 and documents distinctive incised, modeled, slipped, and painted pottery together with platform mounds and evidence of ranked societies.

```text

+
VESSEL
+
RITUAL / FEAST
+

```

**Corpus relation:** material encoding / ceremony / social address.

# 9. SANTARÉM / TAPAJÓ layer

The Smithsonian's *Unknown Amazon* corpus treats Santarém as a major archaeological center for symbolism and power in the tropical forest, alongside Marajoara pottery, Maracá urns, upper Rio Negro histories, Carib basketry, and Amazonian rock art.

```text

-> SYMBOL
-> STATUS / POWER
```

# 12. PEOPLE / identity boundary

Pre-Columbian Amazonia did not leave a surviving local written archive comparable to cuneiform Mesopotamia that securely names ancient rulers, authors, or scientists across these archaeological cultures.

Therefore this layer prioritizes:

```text
NAMED PEOPLE / CULTURES / LANGUAGE NETWORKS
rather than
invented ancient individual biographies
```

Securely documented broad or specific peoples/traditions in museum and ethnohistorical corpora include Tukano and numerous Arawakan, Cariban, Tupi and other language/cultural networks. These labels cover diverse peoples and periods and are not interchangeable.

```text

-> DO NOT INVENT
-> CULTURE / SITE / OBJECT / TRADITION
```


